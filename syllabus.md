## Course Description

The catalog says that students engaged in research or independent study meet with faculty for oral presentations and critical discussion of papers, research plans, and progress, and that seniors using this course for the senior exercise present the results of a research or design project to the seminar.

In practice, this course is about one skill: presenting technical work so that an audience understands it, cares about it, and acts on it. The real deliverable is at the end of the spring semester, when every senior project team gives a ten-minute talk at the department's student conference. A representative of Travelers attends, and prizes go to the best talks in April. This fall builds the skills you will need to be ready for that.

We work through Nancy Duarte's *Resonate* roughly a chapter a week. Duarte's examples come from business and public life; our job is to translate them to technical talks about code, data, and systems. Alongside the reading, you give two talks, write three short pieces, and finish the semester with a preliminary pitch of your senior project to the faculty.

---

## Course Objectives

By the end of the semester you will be able to:

- Plan a talk around what the audience should believe or do afterward, not around what you know
- Structure a technical presentation so that it moves between what is and what could be
- Design slides that support the talk instead of replacing it
- Deliver a ten-minute talk inside the time limit, with a clear opening and a memorable close
- Critique a talk, yours or someone else's, using a shared vocabulary
- Pitch a senior project so that faculty believe it is worth doing

## How the Course Works

### Two talks

**Presentation 1, the "anything" talk (Weeks 3–6).** Ten minutes, alone or with one partner, on any topic you care about: a hobby, a sport, a trip, a side project, an opinion. Senior project teams have not formed yet, so this talk deliberately separates presentation skill from content difficulty. It also lets the rest of us learn who you are.

**Presentation 2, the project pitch (Weeks 8–11).** Ten minutes, given by your senior project team. A first pass at convincing the room that your project is worth doing. Same shape as the preliminary faculty pitch at the end of the semester, lower stakes.

Sign up for a slot on the shared sign-up sheet linked from Moodle. Three slots per section per week. Upload your slides to the Moodle assignment for that round before class on the day you present.

### Three short pieces of writing

All writing in this course is **pass/redo**. There is no numeric grade. If a piece is thin, I send it back once with a note and you have a few days to resubmit.

1. **Talk analysis (due September 22).** Pick a recorded talk of 20 minutes or less, given to a real audience, and write a one-to-two page post-mortem on it in Duarte's vocabulary. This puts the vocabulary in your hands before you present.
2. **Post-mortem 1 (due one week after your Presentation 1).** One page: what you intended, what landed, what did not, what you will change.
3. **Post-mortem 2 (due one week after your Presentation 2).** Same four parts, plus a comparison against what you said you would change after Presentation 1 and against your September talk analysis.

Each assignment has a sheet in the course repository with the full expectations.

### The preliminary faculty pitch

During finals week, each senior project team gives its ten-minute pitch to the faculty. The date will be announced once it is scheduled. The goal is the same as Presentation 2 with a different audience: convince the faculty the project is worth doing. The spring semester ends with the real thing at the student conference.

### Peer feedback and attendance

After every talk, the room asks questions and gives feedback out loud. That feedback is the main way this course works, and it only works if people are in the room. If you are absent on a day a classmate presents, you have taken something from them. Attendance is an obligation to your classmates, not to me.

### Notes on grading

**Talks are graded on the Duarte principles, not on the topic.** Did you know what you wanted the audience to walk away with? Did the talk move? Did the slides help? Did you land inside ten minutes? A talk about your grandmother's recipes that does those things beats a talk about distributed consensus that does not.

**Writing is pass/redo.** A pass is worth one point in the gradebook; three passes is full credit for the category. A piece that comes back for a redo and then passes counts as a pass.

**Post-mortems are due one week after your own talk.** The Moodle due date on each post-mortem is the outer limit for the whole round. Your real deadline is a week after you presented.

**Talks cannot be rescheduled** except for genuine emergencies. If your partner or teammate is absent, the rest of the team presents.

Grade weights are subject to adjustment as the semester develops to ensure fair assignment of course grades.

### Absences and the absence memo

This course meets once a week, so one absence is a large slice of the semester. Classes are recorded on Zoom and the recording link is posted, but a recording is not the same as being in the room.

**If you miss a class, submit a one-page memo on Moodle before the next class.** Summarize the key points covered and the action items, then add one line that is yours alone: the most important takeaway and why, or a question you would have asked. If the class you missed was a presentation day, you may instead write one page of feedback to the students who presented, based on the recording.

You are welcome to use AI to draft the summary from the recording. The personal line is the part that has to be you.

**The memo is what turns an unexcused absence into an excused one.** No memo, and the absence counts against Attendance and Participation. Memos are pass/redo like the other writing.

## Policy on AI Tools

This is a course about presenting. AI tools are welcome for the parts of the work where they help you think and prepare: brainstorming a topic, outlining a talk, drafting and cleaning up slides, rehearsing against a simulated audience, summarizing a recording for an absence memo. Three things stay yours:

- **The talk.** You stand up and deliver it. What you say has to be something you understand and can answer questions about.
- **The post-mortems.** These are about what happened when you presented and what you will change. They are only useful if they are honest and specific to your experience.
- **The personal line in every absence memo.**

When AI substantially shaped something you submit, say so in a sentence. That is not a penalty; it is the same disclosure you would make about any source.

This policy is the course-specific statement that Trinity asks every instructor to provide. It sits inside the college's [AI and Academic Integrity guidelines](https://www.trincoll.edu/academic-ai/students/academic-integrity/) on the [Academic AI site](https://www.trincoll.edu/academic-ai/), which every student should read. The college provides BoodleBox as a secure AI environment, and you are welcome to use it for any of the work above; other tools are fine as well, as long as you do not put other people's private information into them.
